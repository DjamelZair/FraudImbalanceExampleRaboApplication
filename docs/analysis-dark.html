A worked example, built end to end

Where do you
draw the line?

Every dot below is one real card payment. There are 284,807 of them, made over two days, and 492 are fraud.

Finding the fraudulent ones turns out to be the easy part. The hard question is what happens next, because you cannot catch more fraud without flagging more genuine payments alongside it. Flag too few and money walks out of the door. Flag too many and you are interrupting people who did nothing wrong, in the middle of their day.

There is no setting that avoids both. On this page you make that choice yourself, against the model's actual scores, and see exactly what each decision costs. Drag the controls. Nothing here is a mock-up.

I built all of it: a calibrated gradient-boosted model, a four-band response policy and the cost analysis behind it, from 284,807 real payments. On a period it had never seen it flags 64 of the 74 frauds, and the honest summary of its accuracy is a range, 0.68 to 0.86, rather than a single number.

A note on wording

A note on wording. The model flags a payment. It does not block anything and it does not decline anything. What a bank then does with a flag, hold the payment, block the card, send a code, telephone the customer, is an operational decision that belongs to the bank, not to the model. So this page says "flag" and "take action" and deliberately stops there.

You cannot see what anyone bought

28columns hidden behind a PCA transform, labelled only V1–V28 — unreadable by design.
2fields kept in the clear: the amount and the time of each payment.

Payment records are sensitive, so the data was deliberately made unidentifiable before release. Only two fields survive in their original form: the amount of each payment, and the time it happened, counted in seconds from the first transaction in the file.

What V1 to V28 actually are

Everything else, twenty-eight further columns, has been put through a mathematical transformation that scrambles the original details into anonymous numbers called V1 to V28. Those numbers still carry the patterns a model needs, but no one can read a merchant, a location or a cardholder back out of them. It is a real constraint rather than a technicality, and it shows up twice on this page: the model can be accurate, but it cannot explain itself in plain language, and there is nothing in the file about who any of these customers are.

Principal component analysis, and why the model cannot explain itself

The technique is called principal component analysis. Picture a spreadsheet where the columns are things like merchant type, country, hour, distance from the last payment. The method mixes all of those columns together into new columns, each one a different blend of the originals.

The blends keep the structure that matters, so unusual combinations still look unusual to a model, but you can no longer point at a column and say "that one is the country". The original columns are not recoverable from the file. That is the point: the data can be shared for research without exposing anybody's spending.

The practical cost lands later. When the model flags a payment it can tell you which of these blended columns drove the decision, and that is genuinely useful for checking the model, but "V14 was unusually low" is not a sentence you can put in a letter to a customer. On a bank's own data those columns would have real names and real explanations.

The decision

How many payments will you flag?

Because fraud is so rare, a system that waves everything through is right 99.83% of the time and catches nothing at all. So accuracy is the wrong question here. The real question is what it costs when you are wrong, and that depends entirely on which way you are wrong.

How the score works, and what it was tested on

The model gives every payment a risk score and sorts them, most suspicious first. All you have to decide is how far down that list to act. Everything below updates from the real test period: 56,746 payments the model had never seen while it was being built, split off by date rather than at random, because a fraud model always scores payments that happen after the ones it learned from. The four bands were fixed on an earlier period, and this one was scored once.

The first hidden assumption

What does one flag cost you?

From here on, anything written in italics is an assumption rather than something measured. Watch how much of the answer rests on them.

People say a model is "optimised" as though optimal were a property of the model. It is not. To call any setting best you first have to say what a flag costs you and what a missed fraud costs you.

Where the cost of a flag comes from

The missed fraud is easy, because the money is right there in the data. The cost of a flag is not in the data at all. Somebody has to assume it. So here it is, on a slider, where you can see it.

The second hidden assumption

How rare is fraud, really?

Published papers on this dataset regularly report that more than nine out of ten flags are correct. That sounds far better than anything on this page. A good part of the difference is not the model at all. It is that the model was graded on a sample where fraud had been made artificially common.

Why does one number move and the other not?

The two numbers answer different questions, and only one of them depends on how much fraud is in the room.

Of flags
Out of the payments you flagged, how many were really fraud. If you throw away most of the genuine payments before grading, whatever you flag is far more likely to be fraud, purely by arithmetic.
Of all fraud
Out of all the fraud that existed, how much you caught. This is a property of the model itself, so rigging the mix of the sample cannot touch it.

This is worth knowing whenever anyone shows you a fraud model with a spectacular headline number. The first question is not how good the model is. It is what it was graded on.

The part almost nobody reports

Would you get the same answer next month?

Whatever setting you picked was tuned on 74 fraudulent payments. That is not many. If those two days had gone slightly differently, by luck alone, the best answer would have landed somewhere else.

The button below simulates exactly that. It reshuffles the same period into a plausible alternative month and works out the cheapest answer for that month. Press it a few times and see whether the answers agree with each other.

What is being reshuffled, and why does it matter?

If the answer were solid, every dot would land in the same place. They do not. They scatter across a wide range, and the cost assumption has not been touched, so this spread is luck alone.

This is why the notebook does not ship a single tuned number. A threshold picked to look perfect on one month is partly fitted to that month's accidents, and it will not reproduce. The honest output is a range and a policy, not a decimal.

Who carries the cost of your choice

An average is not a person.

Say two customers in a thousand get flagged. Spread evenly, that is nothing anyone would notice. The flags are not spread evenly.

Split the genuine customers by how much they were spending and the burden turns out to land at both ends: on the very smallest payments and on the very largest, while everyone in the middle is left alone. These bars move with your setting from earlier.

How to read these bars
Across
The genuine customers, sorted by payment size and split into ten equal-sized groups. Group 1 is the smallest payments, group 10 the largest. Each group holds roughly the same number of people, so the groups are comparable.
Up
How often people in that group get flagged, compared with the average customer. The dashed line at 1 is the average. A bar at 2 means people in that group are flagged twice as often as average.
Orange bars
Groups flagged clearly more often than average.
Why payment size, and nothing else

Why payment size and not something more direct? No age, no income, no location. There is nothing else in the file to split people by, and payment size is only a rough stand-in for how much someone has to spare.

Why the two mistakes are not the same

One mistake costs money. The other costs trust.

The fraud goes through

A stolen card is used and nothing flags it. The money is gone, the bank refunds it, and somebody spends a week on the paperwork. This cost is real and it is easy to count, which is exactly why it tends to be the only one counted.

The genuine customer gets flagged

An ordinary payment is interrupted. Someone is standing at a till with a queue behind them, or on the phone answering questions about a purchase they were perfectly entitled to make. This cost never appears anywhere in the data, so a model that is not told about it will spend it freely.

This is why one line is the wrong tool

A single cut-off forces every payment into one of two boxes, act or ignore, and that is a poor description of what a bank can actually do. The responses available are not equally costly to the customer. A text message and a blocked card are not the same imposition. So instead of one line, the model's confidence is graded into four bands, and the strength of the response is matched to the strength of the evidence.

One cut-off catching these same 64 frauds would interrupt 2,553 people identically. Grading the response means 114 of them are actually held and the rest get a check costing seconds. The conflict only looks unavoidable while the answer is forced to be yes or no.

What this cannot tell you

The limits are part of the result.

A result quoted without its caveats is a sales pitch.

Two days, not two years

Nothing here shows how the model behaves across seasons, holidays, or a fraud ring changing its methods.

The costs are assumptions

Nobody told me what an interrupted payment costs a customer. You moved that number yourself a moment ago, which is rather the point.

74 fraud cases

Every figure on this page rests on them. That is why the resample button exists, and why the honest headline is a range rather than a number.

Not a fairness audit

The data holds nothing about who these customers are. Payment size stands in for spending power, and it is not the same thing.
