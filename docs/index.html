Where most attempts go wrong

A model that does nothing scores 99.83%.

Because fraud is so rare, a system that waves through every single payment is correct 99.83% of the time. It would be praised in a report and it would catch nothing.

That one fact governs everything that follows. Accuracy is the wrong measure here, and a model that looks excellent on paper can be worthless in practice. So the real question was never how often is it right. It was what does it cost when it is wrong — and that depends entirely on which way it is wrong.

The trade-off at the heart of it

There are two ways to be wrong, and they hurt different people.

Push the system to catch more fraud and it starts stopping honest customers. Ease off to leave customers alone and fraud slips through. You cannot have both. Every fraud system in the world is a position taken on this one tension.

Error one

The fraud gets through

A stolen card is used and nothing stops it. The money is gone. The customer finds out days later, the bank refunds it, and someone spends a week on the paperwork.

Who paysThe bank absorbs the loss, and the customer loses their card, their trust, and an afternoon on the phone.

Error two

The honest customer is stopped

A perfectly normal payment is refused. Someone is standing at a till with a queue behind them and a card that has just been declined, for a purchase they were entitled to make.

Who paysThe customer, immediately and publicly. This is the cost that models tend to ignore, because it never appears in the data.

Most systems resolve this by picking a single line: above it, refuse the payment; below it, allow it. That is the decision I think is wrong.

What I did differently

Four answers instead of two.

A bank is not limited to yes and no. It can send a code to your phone. It can hold a payment for two minutes while somebody looks. Those responses cost a customer almost nothing when they turn out to be unnecessary.

So rather than one line in the sand, the model's confidence is graded into four responses. The stronger the evidence, the more disruptive the response it earns. Being wrong at the bottom of the scale costs a customer three seconds; being wrong at the top costs them a refused payment. So the top is held to a deliberately high standard of proof.

Approve

Nothing happens. The payment goes through as normal and the customer never knows a decision was made.

How certain am I?

The honest answer is a range, not a number.

The test period contained 74 frauds. That is not many, and it means the score would have come out differently on a different two days — through luck alone.

So I measured that luck directly, by re-running the evaluation thousands of times on resampled versions of the same period. Reporting the single number without the range would be claiming a precision the data cannot support.

This matters more than it sounds. If the score drops next month, is the model failing, or was it noise? Without knowing the width of that bar, nobody can tell — and the two situations call for opposite responses.

Who carries the inconvenience

An average hides who is actually being stopped.

Overall, 0.2% of genuine payments were held or refused. But an average is not a person. So I split customers by the size of their payment and checked whether the burden fell evenly.

It did not. The very smallest payments were stopped at more than twice the average rate. A refused payment is not equally inconvenient for everyone, and small purchases tend to belong to people with less room to absorb the trouble.

Finding it is not the same as fixing it. So I also built the fix — a cap that forces the rate back towards the average — and measured what it costs: one extra fraud gets through.

I do not think that decision belongs to me. Whether equal treatment is worth one fraud is a question for the bank and its regulator. What a modeller owes them is the exchange rate, stated plainly and in advance.

What I will not claim

The limits are part of the result.

A result without its caveats is a sales pitch. These are the things this analysis genuinely cannot tell you.

It is two days, not two years

Nothing here shows how the model behaves across seasons, holidays, or a fraud ring changing tactics. That needs months of data.

The costs are my assumptions

Nobody told me what a refused payment costs a customer. I put numbers on it, labelled them as guesses, and showed how the answer changes if they are wrong.

It cannot explain itself to a customer

The data was anonymised for privacy, so the model's reasons are real but unreadable. On genuine data they would be plain sentences.

It is not a fairness audit

The data holds nothing about who these customers are. Payment size is a reasonable stand-in, and it is not the same thing. I would not pretend otherwise.

The strongest model is not automatically the right one to deploy. A threshold tuned to look optimal on 74 cases is tuned to noise. And a system that can only approve or refuse will always resolve this badly.
